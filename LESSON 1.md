## 1. What is Machine Learning?

The lecture begins by contrasting Machine Learning with classical algorithm design.

In a classical algorithmic setting, the designer is typically able to specify the procedure explicitly and understand each step of the computation.

Typical characteristics are:

- mathematical formulation;
- exact or well-defined solutions;
- theoretical guarantees;
- the algorithm designer is "in charge";
- the behavior is predictable from the explicitly written procedure.

Machine Learning changes the perspective:

> instead of specifying every rule explicitly, we let the system improve from **experience / data**.

A classical definition of learning uses three ingredients:

- $E$ = **Experience**
- $T$ = **Task**
- $P$ = **Performance measure**

A system learns from experience $E$ with respect to task $T$ and performance measure $P$ if its performance at $T$, measured by $P$, improves with experience.

### Why $P$ matters

Learning must be measurable.

A system changing its behavior is not enough: we need a quantitative criterion that tells us whether performance is actually improving.

---

## 2. A taxonomy of Machine Learning

### Supervised Learning

We observe labeled examples:

$$
(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)
$$

and want to learn a mapping from inputs to labels.

The input belongs to the **input / feature space**

$$
x\in\mathcal X
$$

and the label belongs to the **label space**

$$
y\in\mathcal Y.
$$

Important cases:

### Binary classification

$$
\mathcal Y=\{0,1\}
$$

### Multi-class classification

$$
\mathcal Y=\{1,\ldots,k\}
$$

### Regression

$$
\mathcal Y=\mathbb R
$$

---

### Unsupervised Learning

We observe only inputs

$$
x_1,\ldots,x_n
$$

without labels.

The goal is to **uncover structure in the data**.

---

### Reinforcement Learning

The lecture only introduces this at a high level.

The basic idea is:

> learn how to act / play through interaction.

No formal development is required at this point.

---

## 3. Why supervised learning is difficult

The lecture highlights several challenges.

Given past labeled examples and a new input $x$, we want to predict the corresponding label $y$.

### 3.1 The phenomenon may be stochastic

The same input does not necessarily determine the label uniquely.

The uncertainty may instead be represented by a conditional distribution such as

$$
P(Y=y\mid X=x).
$$

So, in general, we should not assume that nature follows a deterministic rule

$$
y=g(x).
$$

---

### 3.2 The underlying phenomenon may be arbitrarily complex

Even when a relationship between input and label exists, it may be too complicated to describe directly with a simple explicit rule.

---

### 3.3 We must predict on unseen inputs

The model must make predictions for inputs that were not present in the training data.

This is the **generalization problem**.

> The final objective is not to perform well on the observed sample, but to perform well on future unseen data.

---

### 3.4 Past and future must be related

Learning from past data is meaningful only if future data are generated by a related probabilistic mechanism.

The lecture formalizes this using a distribution

$$
\mathcal D
$$

over

$$
\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y.
$$

---

## 4. The probabilistic model

The central assumption is that examples are sampled from the same underlying distribution.

For the training examples:

$$
(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,(X_n,Y_n)\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal D
$$

and a future example also follows

$$
(X,Y)\sim\mathcal D.
$$

Here **i.i.d.** means:

> **independent and identically distributed**

### Identically distributed

Each example follows the same distribution $\mathcal D$.

### Independent

Different examples do not probabilistically influence one another.

> [!important]
> Independence here concerns **different examples**:
>
> $$
> (X_1,Y_1), (X_2,Y_2),\ldots
> $$
>
> It does **not** mean that $X_i$ and $Y_i$ inside the same example are independent.
>
> In fact, the dependence between input and label is precisely what can make prediction possible.

---

## 5. Spaces, random variables, and realizations

> [!note] Mathematical clarification
> This section makes explicit a distinction that is useful for understanding the course notation.

These are different objects.

### Spaces

$$
\mathcal X
$$

is the input / feature space.

$$
\mathcal Y
$$

is the label space.

---

### Random variables

The random input is denoted by

$$
X
$$

and the random label by

$$
Y.
$$

In a fully formal probability model:

$$
X:\Omega\to\mathcal X
$$

and

$$
Y:\Omega\to\mathcal Y,
$$

where $\Omega$ is the underlying sample space.

---

### Realizations

A realized input is written as

$$
x\in\mathcal X
$$

and a realized label as

$$
y\in\mathcal Y.
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal X,\mathcal Y
\text{ are spaces}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
X,Y
\text{ are random variables}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
x,y
\text{ are realized values}
}
$$

---

## 6. $(X,Y)\sim\mathcal D$ is not a dataset

The notation

$$
(X,Y)\sim\mathcal D
$$

means:

> the random pair $(X,Y)$ has joint probability distribution $\mathcal D$.

It does **not** denote the dataset.

A dataset is a finite collection of realized examples:

$$
S=
\{(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)\}.
$$

Also remember:

$$
x\in\mathcal X
$$

means membership, while

$$
(X,Y)\sim\mathcal D
$$

means "is distributed according to".

---

## 7. Why do we need a joint distribution?

Knowing only the marginal distribution of $Y$ is generally not sufficient for prediction.

Before observing the input, we may know something like

$$
P(Y=1).
$$

After observing

$$
X=x,
$$

the relevant quantity becomes

$$
P(Y=1\mid X=x).
$$

The predictive information lies in the relationship between $X$ and $Y$.

The joint distribution $\mathcal D$ contains the information needed to derive such conditional probabilities.

For discrete random variables:

$$
P(Y=y\mid X=x)
=
\frac{P(X=x,Y=y)}{P(X=x)}.
$$

Equivalently:

$$
P(X=x,Y=y)
=
P(Y=y\mid X=x)P(X=x).
$$

---

## 8. Informative features

If

$$
P(Y\mid X=x)=P(Y)
$$

for every $x$, then observing $X=x$ provides no additional information about $Y$.

In that case, $X$ and $Y$ are independent and the features are not useful for predicting the label.

> [!important]
> A **joint distribution does not imply dependence**.
>
> A joint distribution describes the probabilistic relationship between $X$ and $Y$, whether they are dependent or independent.

---

## 9. Training, validation, and test sets

The lecture distinguishes three roles.

### Training set

Used to **fit / train the model**.

### Validation set

Used to **compare models** and select configurations / hyperparameters.

### Test set

Used for the **final evaluation** of the complete Machine Learning pipeline.

A useful summary is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Training}\rightarrow\text{fit}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Validation}\rightarrow\text{choose}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Test}\rightarrow\text{evaluate}
}
$$

The lecture also mentions **$k$-fold cross-validation** as a practical validation strategy.

---

## 10. Decision Theory

The lecture then asks:

> once we observe an input $x$, how should we decide what to predict?

This is where Decision Theory enters.

### Probability vs Decision Theory

> [!important]
> **Probability describes uncertainty. Decision Theory turns uncertainty into a decision.**

Probability gives quantities such as

$$
P(Y=y\mid X=x).
$$

This answers:

> Given $x$, how likely are the possible labels?

But a predictor must output a concrete prediction.

Decision Theory adds a notion of **cost** for each possible prediction error.

---

## 11. Predictor

A predictor is a function

$$
f:\mathcal X\to\mathcal Y.
$$

For a realized input $x$,

$$
f(x)
$$

is the prediction.

Important distinction:

- $Y$ = random true label;
- $f(X)$ = random predicted label;
- $y$ = realized true label;
- $f(x)$ = realized prediction.

---

## 12. Loss function

A loss function measures how bad a prediction is.

The current formal handout uses the convention

$$
\ell:\mathcal Y\times\mathcal Y\to\mathbb R
$$

with

$$
\ell(\text{prediction},\text{truth}).
$$

So the loss on one realized example is

$$
\ell(f(x),y).
$$

### 0-1 loss

Used in classification:

$$
\ell(a,b)=\mathbf 1\{a\neq b\}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\ell(a,b)=
\begin{cases}
0 & a=b\\
1 & a\neq b.
\end{cases}
$$

---

### Squared loss

Common in regression:

$$
\ell(a,b)=(a-b)^2.
$$

Large errors are penalized aggressively because the error is squared.

---

### Absolute loss

$$
\ell(a,b)=|a-b|.
$$

Errors are penalized linearly.

---

### Asymmetric losses

In some applications, different mistakes may have different costs.

For binary classification, false negatives and false positives need not be equally costly.

This is why the loss function is a **modeling choice**.

---

## 13. Distribution vs loss

This distinction is central.

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal D
=
\text{how the environment generates data}
}
$$

while

$$
\boxed{
\ell
=
\text{how we evaluate predictions}
}
$$

The distribution is determined by the environment.

The loss function is chosen by the model designer according to the application.

---

## 14. Expected risk

The loss

$$
\ell(f(x),y)
$$

evaluates one realized prediction.

To evaluate the predictor globally, we take the expected loss over the data-generating distribution.

The expected risk is

$$
\boxed{
R_{\mathcal D}(f)
=
\mathbb E_{(X,Y)\sim\mathcal D}
[\ell(f(X),Y)]
}
$$

When the dependence on $\mathcal D$ is clear, we may simply write

$$
R(f).
$$

### Interpretation

> The expected risk is the average loss we expect the predictor to incur on data generated according to $\mathcal D$.

It is a **population / distribution-level quantity**, not an average over the observed training dataset.

---

## 15. Discrete form of the expected risk

If $X$ and $Y$ are discrete:

$$
R(f)
=
\sum_{x\in\mathcal X}
\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}
\ell(f(x),y)
P(X=x,Y=y).
$$

This is just the expected-value rule:

$$
\text{value}\times\text{probability},
$$

summed over every possible input-label pair.

---

## 16. Lecture example

The lecture considers

$$
\mathcal X=\{1,2,3\},
\qquad
\mathcal Y=\{0,1\},
$$

with predictor

$$
f(1)=1,\qquad
f(2)=0,\qquad
f(3)=1.
$$

Using 0-1 loss, the classifier is wrong on the logical pairs

$$
(1,0),\quad(2,1),\quad(3,0).
$$

However,

$$
P(X=1,Y=0)=0,
$$

so that error contributes zero to the expected risk.

The two positive-probability errors each have probability

$$
\frac{1}{12}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
R(f)
=
0+\frac{1}{12}+\frac{1}{12}
=
\boxed{\frac16}.
$$

With 0-1 loss:

$$
R(f)=P(f(X)\neq Y).
$$

So the risk is the probability of misclassification under the underlying distribution.

---

## 17. Expected risk is not empirical risk

> [!important]
> Do not confuse the expected risk with the average loss on the observed sample.

Expected risk:

$$
R_{\mathcal D}(f)
=
\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}[\ell(f(X),Y)].
$$

It depends on the underlying distribution $\mathcal D$.

A finite training set contains only realized examples sampled from that distribution.

The empirical-risk distinction becomes explicit in **Lecture 2**, where it will be studied properly.

## Key formulas

### Joint / conditional relation

$$
P(X=x,Y=y)
=
P(Y=y\mid X=x)P(X=x).
$$

### Predictor

$$
f:\mathcal X\to\mathcal Y.
$$

### Realized loss

$$
\ell(f(x),y).
$$

### Expected risk

$$
R_{\mathcal D}(f)
=
\mathbb E_{(X,Y)\sim\mathcal D}
[\ell(f(X),Y)].
$$

### Discrete expected risk

$$
R(f)
=
\sum_x\sum_y
\ell(f(x),y)P(X=x,Y=y).
$$

---

## Pitfalls

### 1. $\mathcal X$ is not the dataset

$\mathcal X$ is the set of all possible input values.

The dataset contains a finite number of realizations:

$$
x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\mathcal X.
$$

### 2. A distribution is not a set of samples

$\mathcal D$ is a probability distribution.

The training set is a finite sample drawn according to $\mathcal D$.

### 3. Joint distribution does not mean dependence

$X$ and $Y$ may have a joint distribution and still be independent.

### 4. i.i.d. does not mean $X_i$ and $Y_i$ are independent

Independence is between different examples:

$$
(X_i,Y_i)
$$

and

$$
(X_j,Y_j),
\quad i\neq j.
$$

### 5. Risk is not training error

Expected risk is defined over the underlying distribution.

Training error is computed on a finite observed sample.

### 6. Logical error vs contribution to risk

A predictor may be wrong on an input-label combination whose probability is zero.

Such a pair contributes

$$
0
$$

to expected risk.
