# Random Variables

## Core Idea

A **random variable** is a function that assigns a numerical value to each outcome of a random experiment.

Formally:

$$
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
$$

where:

- $\Omega$ is the [[Sample Space]];
- $\omega\in\Omega$ is a single **outcome**;
- $X$ is the random variable;
- $X(\omega)$ is the numerical value assigned by $X$ to the outcome $\omega$.

---

## Outcome vs Event — Important

Do not confuse an **outcome** with an **event**.

### Outcome

An **outcome** is one single possible result of the experiment.

It is an element of the sample space:

$$
\boxed{\omega\in\Omega}
$$

For a die:

$$
\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
$$

the result:

$$
\omega=4
$$

is an **outcome**.

### Event

An **event** is a set of outcomes.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{A\subseteq\Omega}
$$

For example:

$$
A=\{2,4,6\}
$$

is the event:

> "The die shows an even number."

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{outcome}=\text{one element of }\Omega
}
$$

while:

$$
\boxed{
\text{event}=\text{a set of outcomes}
}
$$

### Memory Check

For:

$$
\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
$$

we have:

$$
4\in\Omega
$$

because $4$ is an **outcome**.

But:

$$
\{2,4,6\}\subseteq\Omega
$$

because $\{2,4,6\}$ is an **event**.

Think:

> **Outcome = one possible result.**  
> **Event = a collection of possible results satisfying some condition.**

---

## Why Do We Need Random Variables?

The outcomes themselves do not necessarily have the numerical meaning we want to study.

A random variable extracts a numerical quantity from an outcome.

For example, toss two coins:

$$
\Omega=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}.
$$

Define:

$$
X=\text{number of heads}.
$$

Then:

$$
X(HH)=2
$$

$$
X(HT)=1
$$

$$
X(TH)=1
$$

$$
X(TT)=0.
$$

The random variable maps:

$$
\boxed{
\text{outcome}
\xrightarrow{X}
\text{numerical value}
}
$$

---

## Random Variable vs Its Value

Another important distinction:

$$
X
$$

is the **function**.

While:

$$
X(\omega)
$$

is the **value produced by that function for a particular outcome**.

For example:

$$
\omega=HT
$$

is an outcome.

Then:

$$
X(HT)=1.
$$

So:

- $HT$ is the outcome;
- $X$ is the random variable;
- $1$ is the value assigned by $X$.

---

## The Event $\{X=x\}$

An expression such as:

$$
X=x
$$

identifies all outcomes that the random variable maps to $x$.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\{X=x\}\text{ is an event}}
$$

because it represents a set of outcomes.

For the two-coin example:

$$
\{X=1\}=\{HT,TH\}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(X=1)
=
P(\{HT,TH\})
=
\frac{2}{4}
=
\frac12.
$$

The probability is ultimately still being assigned to an **event**.

---

## Complete Picture

Using a die:

$$
\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
$$

define:

$$
X(\omega)=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if }\omega\text{ is even}\\
0 & \text{if }\omega\text{ is odd}.
\end{cases}
$$

Then:

### Sample Space

$$
\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
$$

contains every possible outcome.

### Outcome

$$
\omega=4
$$

is one specific outcome.

### Random Variable

$$
X:\Omega\rightarrow\{0,1\}
$$

maps outcomes to numerical values.

### Value of the Random Variable

$$
X(4)=1.
$$

### Event Generated by the Random Variable

$$
\{X=1\}=\{2,4,6\}.
$$

### Probability of That Event

$$
P(X=1)
=
P(\{2,4,6\})
=
\frac36
=
0.5.
$$

Therefore the hierarchy is:

$$
\boxed{
\Omega
\rightarrow
\omega
\rightarrow
X(\omega)
}
$$

while conditions such as:

$$
\boxed{
\{X=x\}\subseteq\Omega
}
$$

define events.

---

## Discrete Random Variables

A **discrete random variable** takes values from a finite or countable set.

For example:

$$
X\in\{0,1,2\}.
$$

Its probability distribution can be described by a **Probability Mass Function (PMF)**:

$$
\boxed{
p_X(x)=P(X=x)
}
$$

For the number of heads in two coin tosses:

| $x$ | $P(X=x)$ |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | $1/4$ |
| 1 | $1/2$ |
| 2 | $1/4$ |

---

## Continuous Random Variables

A **continuous random variable** can take values over a continuous range.

Examples include quantities such as:

- height;
- temperature;
- time;
- distance.

Continuous random variables are described using a **Probability Density Function (PDF)**.

An important difference from the discrete case is that, for a continuous random variable:

$$
P(X=x)=0.
$$

Probabilities are instead assigned to intervals, for example:

$$
P(a\le X\le b).
$$

This will be developed when needed.

## Connection to Machine Learning

Random variables are fundamental in Machine Learning.

Typically:

$$
X=\text{input/features}
$$

and:

$$
Y=\text{target/output}.
$$

This leads to expressions such as:

$$
P(Y=y\mid X=x),
$$

which asks for the probability of a target value given observed input features.

Later we will also encounter:

$$
\mathbb E[X],
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X),
$$

$$
P(X,Y),
$$

and:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).
$$

These require understanding $X$ and $Y$ as random variables.

## What I Should Never Confuse

$$
\boxed{
\omega\in\Omega
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\omega\text{ is an outcome}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
A\subseteq\Omega
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
A\text{ is an event}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb R
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
X\text{ is a random variable}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
X(\omega)
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{value assigned to outcome }\omega
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\{X=x\}\subseteq\Omega
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\{X=x\}\text{ is an event}
}
$$

## Active Recall

Given a sample space $\Omega$ and a random variable $X$, I should be able to identify:

1. What is the sample space?
2. What is an individual outcome $\omega$?
3. What is an event?
4. What function does $X$ represent?
5. What does $X(\omega)$ represent?
6. Why is $\{X=x\}$ an event?
7. What does $P(X=x)$ actually mean in terms of outcomes?
